Pen or Keyboard? What Happens When Students Take Notes

Hana Ortiz · Approach Learning Review, 4(2), 12–14 · 2026

1Most university students now take notes on a laptop. Typing is fast, so students can record almost every word a lecturer says. But is more always better?

2In a study at one university, researchers divided 120 first-year students into two groups. One group took notes by hand; the other typed. After a 20-minute lecture, the typists had written almost twice as many words. However, many of their notes copied the lecture word for word.

3One week later, both groups took a test. On questions about facts, such as dates and names, the two groups scored about the same. On questions about concepts — explaining why or how — the handwriting group scored 15% higher. Because handwriting is slower, students cannot write down everything, so they must decide what is important and put it in their own words.

4Typing still has advantages. Typed notes are easy to search, share and organize, and they are useful in long lectures with many details.

5The author's advice is simple: the tool matters less than the method. Whether you use a pen or a keyboard, write the main ideas in your own words and review your notes within 24 hours. The study was small and took place at only one university, so more research is needed.

The Power of Short Breaks at Work

Marcus Lee · Workplace Today · 3 March 2025

1Many office workers skip breaks because they feel too busy. They believe that working without stopping means getting more done. New survey data suggest the opposite.

2Workplace Today surveyed 800 office workers in five countries. Workers who took a five-minute break every hour reported 20% less tiredness at the end of the day than workers who took no breaks. They also completed 8% more tasks per week, according to their own records.

3The type of break matters. Breaks spent walking, stretching or talking to a colleague helped the most. Breaks spent looking at a phone helped much less, because the brain does not really rest.

4Some managers worry that breaks waste time. However, the data suggest that a few short breaks cost less time than the mistakes tired workers make.

5There are limits. The workers reported their own tiredness and tasks, so the results may not be fully accurate. Still, the advice is easy to try: plan short, screen-free breaks, and protect them like meetings.
